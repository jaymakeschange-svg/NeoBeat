# Build a stereo room from permissive parts

Everything the stereo room needs can be written from scratch, and should be: for 4–16 elements rendered to ordinary stereo, the entire placement engine is a pair of time-varying gains per element, a side-channel high-pass for the mono-locked ones, a small inter-channel delay for the ones that can take it, and **one shared reverb send** that is O(1) in element count. The two techniques that sound like the obvious answer — HRTF binaural and ambisonics — are both wrong for this product on documented grounds, not taste: binaural cues are destroyed by loudspeaker crosstalk and recovering them needs a sweet spot of about **±4°** ([audioXpress](https://audioxpress.com/article/audioscenic-amphi-technology-high-dimensional-sound)), while ambisonics adds an encode/decode round-trip and a normalisation convention to get wrong in exchange for capabilities a stereo deliverable cannot cash in ([Wikipedia: Ambisonic data exchange formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambisonic_data_exchange_formats)). The visualisation side is a solved design problem with a stable vocabulary — pan on X, level as glyph size or brightness, width as a property of the glyph rather than an axis, history as per-frame fade plus per-age blur — and for sixteen translucent blobs the lowest-risk renderer in JUCE 8 is plain `Graphics`/`Path` with painter's-algorithm sorting, because *both* GPU paths have documented multi-instance plug-in failures. The real risks in this build are not CPU and not licensing; they are four specific traps: JUCE's `dsp::Panner` normalises to the **centre** rather than the extremes, so hard-panned elements read 3–6 dB hot against a textbook panner; `SmoothedValue::setTargetValue` restarts the whole ramp on every call, turning JUCE's own 50 ms house ramp into a bouncing chase at ordinary buffer sizes; VST 3 forbids changing the parameter set through reconfiguration, so the element bank must be fixed at 16 from version one; and SAF's `saf_tracker` module flips that entire otherwise-permissive framework to GPLv2 if it is left enabled. Four published formulas encountered in this research are wrong and are named below so they are not implemented.

## Amplitude panning is the whole engine, and binaural fails on speakers

Three pan laws dominate, and the arithmetic is settled. With pan angle θ ∈ [0, π/2] and centre at π/4, the **linear** law is L = (π/2 − θ)(2/π), R = θ(2/π), giving 0.5 per channel at centre (**−6 dB per channel**, −3 dB power); **constant power** is L = cos θ, R = sin θ, giving ≈0.71 (**−3 dB per channel**, 0 dB power); and the **−4.5 dB compromise** is the geometric mean of the two, L = √((π/2 − θ)(2/π)·cos θ), giving ≈0.59 ([CMU, Loudness Concepts & Panning Laws](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~music/icm-online/readings/panlaws/)). The trade is entirely about mono summing: linear sums to unity so centre material is not boosted, but "sounds softer at the center"; constant power holds power flat across the arc at the cost of **up to 3 dB** of centre boost when summed; the −4.5 dB law caps that at **1.5 dB**. The same page records that Disney's 1930s cinema tests favoured constant power while the BBC's 1950s TV tests favoured −4.5 dB, and concludes "there is no universal solution that optimizes panning" ([CMU](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~music/icm-online/readings/panlaws/)). For a per-element placement plugin whose output must survive a mono fold, **−4.5 dB is the right default** because it bounds the error at 1.5 dB; linear is the only law that is exactly mono-neutral. One caution about that source: it states per-channel attenuation as −4.5 dB while its formula writes 10·log₁₀ where 20·log₁₀ applies to amplitudes, and it never defines a separate "−6 dB law" — its "−3 dB" refers to constant-power centre attenuation.

JUCE's shipping implementation is usable under the commercial licence and worth reading, but it **does not follow the textbook reference point**, and this is the discrepancy most likely to produce a wrong meter. `juce::dsp::Panner::update()` clamps pan to [−1,1], computes `normalisedPan = 0.5 * (pan + 1)`, and multiplies each channel value by a per-rule `boostValue` ([docs.juce.com dsp::Panner](https://docs.juce.com/master/classdsp_1_1Panner.html)):

| Rule | left | right | boost | centre | hard pan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `balanced` / default | min(0.5, 1−n) | min(0.5, n) | 2 | 0 dB | +6 dB |
| `linear` | 1−n | n | 2 | 0 dB | +6 dB |
| `sin3dB` | sin(0.5π(1−n)) | sin(0.5π·n) | √2 | 0 dB | +3 dB |
| `sin4p5dB` | same, ^1.5 | same, ^1.5 | 2^(3/4) | 0 dB | +4.5 dB |
| `sin6dB` | same, ^2 | same, ^2 | 2 | 0 dB | +6 dB |
| `squareRoot3dB` | √(1−n) | √n | √2 | 0 dB | +3 dB |
| `squareRoot4p5dB` | √(1−n)^1.5 | √n^1.5 | 2^(3/4) | 0 dB | +4.5 dB |

Working the table (this is the researcher's derivation, not a quoted claim): at n = 0.5 every rule yields exactly unity — `sin3dB` gives 0.7071 × √2 = 1.0, `sin4p5dB` gives 0.7071^1.5 × 2^0.75 = 1.0 — so **JUCE holds the centre at 0 dB and lets the extremes rise by +3, +4.5 or +6 dB**, where the textbook presentation holds the extremes at 0 dB and drops the centre. Same law, opposite reference point. Pick one convention for the whole product and state it in the UI, or hard-panned blobs will read hot by 3–6 dB against any external reference. Two further notes from the same source reading: `balanced` is a **balance control, not a panner** (the min(0.5, …) clamp holds one channel at unity), so it is right for rebalancing an already-stereo element and wrong for placing a mono one; and because `sin4p5dB` is just the sine law raised to 1.5, a single `lawExponent` with k = 1 → 3 dB, 1.5 → 4.5 dB, 2 → 6 dB gives a continuously variable pan law for the price of one `pow`.

For a draggable 2D puck, the principled mapping from (x, y) to gains is VBAP reduced to the stereo pair, which is exactly the tangent law. Pulkki's formulation is p = g₁l₁ + g₂l₂ (Eq. 7) solved as g = pᵀ·L₁₂⁻¹ (Eq. 9), with the constant-power condition g₁² + g₂² = C (Eq. 1) and scaling g_scaled = √C·g/√(g₁²+g₂²) (Eq. 10); the paper's appendix shows the Eq. 9 gains satisfy the **tangent law** tan φ / tan φ₀ = (g₁−g₂)/(g₁+g₂), described as "more correct" when the listener turns the head, while the **law of sines** is the fixed-head assumption ([Pulkki 1997](https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/resources/aps-w23/papers/sap_Pulkki1997.pdf)). The payoff for this product is that the puck's position becomes an **angle inside the real speaker base** rather than an arbitrary normalised pan, leaving y free to drive distance. Pulkki's rule that a negative gain factor must be zeroed before normalisation matters even in stereo: it is what stops a source dragged outside the base from contributing a polarity-inverted signal, which would be a mono-compatibility failure rather than a localisation error. One knob unifies all of this across channel counts — SuperCollider's documented `levelCompFactor = numChans ** -p` with p clipped to [0,1], p = 1 giving 1/n (safe for highly correlated signals) and p = 0.5 giving 1/√n (for weakly correlated ones), attributed to Laitinen et al. 2014 ([SuperCollider: Level Compensation](https://docs.supercollider.online/Guides/Level_Compensation.html)).

Binaural and ambisonics should both stay out of the main path. Over two loudspeakers each channel reaches both ears, which "smears the binaural cues the content creator intended", and binaural cues are "distorted to the point that 3D cues cannot be perceived as intended"; crosstalk cancellation dates to Atal and Schroeder in 1962, requires a fixed listening position where "a deviation of as little as 5 cm sharply reduces spatial perception", cancelled substantially only within **about ±4° of centre** in a static system, and introduces spectral colouration because stereo HRTFs are strongly inter-ear correlated at low frequencies ([audioXpress](https://audioxpress.com/article/audioscenic-amphi-technology-high-dimensional-sound)). That article is written by Audioscenic's CTO and its 15-participant preference test is self-reported company data with no stated methodology, so the product comparison is not reliable; the physics claims are standard and consistent with the wider literature. Steam Audio's own binaural effect ships a `spatialBlend` control mixing spatialised with unspatialised audio, which is itself evidence that full-wet HRTF is often unwanted ([Steam Audio C API guide](https://valvesoftware.github.io/steam-audio/doc/capi/guide.html)). Ambisonics costs (N+1)² channels — 16 at third order — plus the SN3D-versus-N3D distinction, where the two differ only by a 1/√(2l+1) scaling per degree "so mixing them can produce errors that are hard to hear" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambisonic_data_exchange_formats)). Note that this Wikipedia page carries its own accuracy flag — it is marked as needing review by a mathematics expert and its conversion table is marked under construction — so if an ambisonic I/O mode is ever added, the factors must be checked against Zotter & Frank or the ambiX paper first. If a headphone mode ships at all, it should be explicitly labelled, default off, and bundle MIT KEMAR's **128-tap compact set** (2 × 128 = 256 MACs/sample/source, ≈12.3 M MAC/s at 48 kHz — the researcher's arithmetic from the published tap counts, not a benchmark) with optional user SOFA loading.

## Mono-locking a kick means forbidding width, not summing its output

The reason bass is kept mono is physical on vinyl and practical everywhere else: a bass-to-mono processor high-passes the **Side** signal so low-frequency *vertical* groove modulation becomes lateral, which keeps the stylus from being pinched out of the groove, and the filtering creates a complementary low-frequency crosstalk curve that reduces LF separation ([KA Electronics EEQ-12](https://www.ka-electronics.com/kaelectronics/Elliptic_EQ/Elliptic_EQ.htm)). Published crossovers cluster tightly: hardware elliptic EQ offers relay-selectable **75 / 150 / 300 Hz** at a default **12 dB/octave**, switchable to 6/6 dB/oct to emulate the Neumann EE-70/EE-77 ([KA Electronics](https://www.ka-electronics.com/kaelectronics/Elliptic_EQ/Elliptic_EQ.htm)); plugin practice uses **100 Hz** with "second-order filters with 12 dB/octave slopes" as typical, implemented either as M/S with the S path high-passed or as low-passed crosstalk between channels (−15 dB at 200 Hz, −27 dB at 400 Hz for a 100 Hz corner) ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/sound-advice/q-how-do-mono-maker-plug-ins-work)); and mixing guidance converges on **roughly 150 Hz** — keep everything below centred, pan the upper band — with the added argument that centring low frequencies lets "both speakers share the load of reproducing them" ([Sound On Sound, Making The Most Of The Stereo Panorama](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/making-most-stereo-panorama)). **120–150 Hz is the defensible default** for the crossover variant.

The crucial design point is that there are two different features here. A full mono lock is simply S = 0, which leaves L = R = M bit-exact with no filter and therefore **no transition region to smear** — for a kick or a bass this is strictly better than a crossover. The crossover variant is for elements whose *source* is stereo (stereo kick layers, DI plus amp, an 808 with stereo reverb), where a side high-pass removes the problematic content and leaves the stereo character of the upper harmonics intact. And for an element whose source is already mono, S = 0 by construction, so panning it with any law keeps it phase-coherent — which means **"keep the kick mono" in a per-element plugin means forbidding the width and delay stages on that element, not summing its output.** Both the mixing and the manufacturer sources go further and say the lock should be the *default* for low-frequency elements: Cableguys' own manual suggests keeping lows mono and centred while widening the highs, and warns that Haas panning can cause phasing in mono, recommending a mono check, band-limiting, or a WidthShaper at 0% ([Cableguys ShaperBox 3 Manual](https://www.cableguys.com/downloads/Cableguys-ShaperBox-3-Manual.pdf?v=3.3.1)). KA Electronics' hardware puts a **phase-correcting network in the Mid path** specifically to compensate the Side filter's phase response; the digital equivalent is a matched all-pass in M, or simply running a Linkwitz-Riley crossover and mono-ing only the low band, which sums to all-pass by construction.

Width and widening sit on a clean cost ranking that follows from the M/S identity 2L = M + S, 2R = M − S ([audiosignal.co.uk, Stereo shuffling](https://www.audiosignal.co.uk/Resources/Stereo_shuffling_A4.pdf)). A single width coefficient W applied as L′ = M + W·S, R′ = M − W·S sums to 2M **for any W** — the ±W·S terms cancel exactly — so M/S width is the only widener with zero mono cost in the strict sum sense, and for the same reason it cannot widen a mono source at all. Its real hazards are the stereo-to-mono level shift of widened material and clipping, since |L′| can reach |M| + W|S|, which argues for exposing a compensating trim. Blumlein-style shuffling restricts the boost to low frequencies, with a transition around **600 Hz** attributed to Griesinger rather than Blumlein and a recommended **4–8 dB** boost of S relative to M below it; the same source gives the S/M-gain-to-crosstalk mapping (a 1 dB change of S ↔ −24.8 dB crosstalk, 20 dB ↔ −1.7 dB), accurate only when S has no phase shift relative to M. That caveat is the general warning in miniature: all-pass and delay-based wideners buy width with inter-channel phase, and phase is exactly what mono summing destroys. A delay τ puts mono-sum nulls at f = (2k+1)/(2τ), so **τ = 1 ms nulls at 500 Hz, 10 ms at 50 Hz, 0.3 ms at 1.67 kHz** — short delays wreck the mono sum inside the musical band, which is why delay widening belongs on hats, shakers, guitars and pads, never on kick or bass. The resulting ranking, from zero cost upward: M/S width, then LF-only shuffling, then all-pass decorrelation (broadband colouration of the mono sum), then short-delay Haas (deep combing), then polarity-inverted tricks (near-total cancellation). Mixing practice agrees from the other direction: time-delay methods "cause comb filtering and a phasey sound in mono", M/S wideners keep the mono balance mostly intact but the mono listener hears only the Mid so the balance can shift, and polarity-inverted widening avoids combing but changes levels ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/making-most-stereo-panorama)).

Metering should be per-element and per-band, not one broadband number. The correlation meter is the Pearson coefficient of L and R over a sliding window — a readable open implementation keeps the last N pairs (**default 4096**), computes means in f64, accumulates the centred cross-product and both centred sums of squares, divides by √(var_L·var_R) and clamps to [−1, +1], with a **rectangular** window, no smoothing of the output value, and silence or constant signals forced to **0.0** rather than treated as an error ([oximedia-metering correlation.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/oximedia-metering/latest/source/src/correlation.rs)) — though that crate's licence was not visible on the fetched page, so read it for the algorithm only. An equivalent one-pole form with O(1) state (smooth L·R, L·L and R·R separately, then divide) is what the Faust/HISE community uses, with the first-order cutoff derived from an integration time as `lpf_freq = 1/(2π·integration_time)`, defaulting to **0.05 s** over a 0.01–0.2 s range and an epsilon of 1e-10 in the denominator ([HISE forum](https://forum.hise.audio/topic/10913/correlation)). **One formula in that same thread must not be implemented**: a poster proposes `Correlation = arctan(L/R) · √(L² + R²)` and calls it normalised — it is not bounded to [−1, +1] and the arctan term does not measure correlation.

Correlation also does not measure what the reader actually needs. Because r is normalised by each channel's own energy, it is blind to level: a hard-left mono source reads 0 (one channel has zero variance), and two unrelated sources also read ≈0, yet their mono behaviour is completely different. **r is a cancellation indicator, not a mono-compatibility measure.** The honest readout is a mono-fold level delta in dB, and it is cheap because expanding the sum gives `monoPower = P_L + P_R + 2r·√(P_L·P_R)`, which for equal-power channels collapses to `2P(1 + r)` — so mono gain relative to the in-phase case is √((1+r)/2): r = 1 → 0 dB, r = 0 → −3 dB, r = −0.5 → −6 dB, r → −1 → total cancellation. That identity is a **derivation from the cited correlation definition**, not a quoted result, and it is the cleanest way to put a correlation reading into dB on screen. Run it per band (3–8 filterbank outputs or FFT bins) so a negative r confined below 150 Hz is visible instead of averaged away — the bass problem is precisely what a broadband meter hides. No IEC or EBU specification of correlation-meter ballistics, integration time or band limits was located, so the 4096-sample window and any banding thresholds are one implementation's choices, not a standard.

## Depth comes from one shared reverb send, not from air absorption

The distance cue hierarchy is the opposite of what a physical model suggests. Direct-to-reverberant ratio is named "a key distance cue", with more distant sources relying more on reflections than on the direct wave; a point source's amplitude falls as **1/r**, about **6 dB per doubling**; and the engine vendor states plainly that production curves deliberately depart from physics — dialogue often gets little or flat attenuation, real sources span ~0–190 dB SPL where playback cannot, and game worlds are visually scaled ([Audiokinetic, A Wwise Approach to Spatial Audio](https://audiokinetic.com/a-wwise-approach-to-spatial-audio-part-1)). The same source is blunt that high-passing with distance is a **mixing convention, not physics** — it exists because sending more signal to reverb muddies the low end, and acoustics texts do not describe low-frequency energy loss with distance — and documents the deliberately non-physical designer tweak of making reflections *quieter* close to the source and *louder* far away, which is what makes small distance moves audible. Wwise simulates up to four reflections per path and gives its Reflect bus its own distance curves separate from the source's attenuation; it publishes no recommended default curves, and the terms "spread" and "focus" are named but not defined in that article.

Air absorption is real, measured, and almost irrelevant at mix distances — and the one open-source coefficient set found for it contains a value that must not be copied. Measured attenuation at 20 °C (dB/km, with per-metre conversions) is **5.6 dB/km at 1 kHz** across humidities, rising to 16 at 2 kHz, 30 at 3 kHz and **105 dB/km at 4 kHz** at 40% RH, falling to 63 at 4 kHz by 70% RH, with dB per metre = m × 4.343 from the attenuation constant ([Smith, *Physical Audio Signal Processing*, Air Absorption](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp05/Air_Absorption.html)); the filter framing replaces the unit delay z⁻¹ with a propagation filter G(z) constrained to |G| ≤ 1, so loss scales with delay length through the exponent M and is **exponential in r** where spreading is hyperbolic ([Smith, PASP, Frequency-Dependent Air Absorption Filtering](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp04/Frequency_Dependent_Air_Absorption_Filtering.html)). Steam Audio's Apache-2.0 Rust rewrite implements `evaluate(distance, band)` as `exp(-coefficient[band] * distance)` with three default coefficients read as **`[0.0002, 0.0017, 0.182]`** ([phonon air_absorption.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/phonon/latest/source/src/models/air_absorption.rs)). **That third value is flagged as inconsistent and should not be used as read:** 0.182 gives exp(−0.182 × 100) ≈ −158 dB at 100 m, wildly at odds with the measured ~10 dB/100 m at 4 kHz, whereas **0.0182** gives ≈−15.8 dB at 100 m, which is consistent. Treat it as a transcription issue — in the rewrite or in the fetched rendering — pending verification against upstream Steam Audio source, which was not retrieved; the band centre frequencies were not visible either. The practical consequence is worth stating to users rather than hiding: at 0.105 dB/m at 4 kHz, a 10 m "distant" placement loses about **1 dB** at 4 kHz, so the audible "distance tone change" in a music plugin is substantially an artistic exaggeration, exactly as the Wwise material says about designer curves. Do not sell it as a physical model. The perceptually validated alternative is cruder still: the RAZR hybrid room model approximates air absorption with **a single fixed 2nd-order Butterworth bandpass, 20 Hz to 14 kHz**, whose high cutoff stands in for the whole phenomenon ([Wendt, van de Par & Ewert, JAES 2014](https://medi.uni-oldenburg.de/razr/files/Wendt_et_al_2014_JAES.pdf)).

That paper is the strongest available answer to "what does a convincing room actually need", and its numbers set the architecture. A **3rd-order shoebox image-source model (63 image sources)** feeding a **12-delay-line FDN** with a randomly generated orthogonal feedback matrix, delays scaled to room dimensions with jitter, and absorption filters set from **Sabine's formula**, was rated by 15 listeners as **not significantly different in naturalness from measured BRIRs** for N > 0, with some synthesised conditions rated slightly more natural; T60 matched measurements well, EDT deviated by roughly 2–10 JNDs, and interaural cross-correlation matched well except at N = 0 ([Wendt et al. 2014](https://medi.uni-oldenburg.de/razr/files/Wendt_et_al_2014_JAES.pdf)). The ISM is cheap precisely because it is restricted to empty shoeboxes with homogeneous per-wall absorption, "which avoids visibility tests", and wall reflection coefficients are specified in octave bands from 250 Hz to 4 kHz. Two findings in it are decisive for a 16-element plugin. First, "**even N = 0 may suffice for localization because of the precedence effect**" — but with N = 0 the interaural cross-correlation was too low and the result sounded "more metallic". Localisation and spatial *quality* are separable budgets: the direct sound and the precedence effect do the positional work, and early reflections buy coherence and timbre. Second, "**ISM cost grows with the cube of the RIR duration while FDN cost is fixed**" — so per-element cost must be the early part, kept to order 1, and the expensive diffuse tail must be a **shared send-style FDN**. That matches Steam Audio's documented behaviour, where real-time reflections carry "significant CPU usage cost" but **reverb cost does not depend on the number of sources** ([Steam Audio C API guide](https://valvesoftware.github.io/steam-audio/doc/capi/guide.html)). Per-element reverb instances are what will blow the budget. Two caveats on transferring Wendt's numbers: the work targets BRIRs for headphones, so its per-image HRIR spatialisation and its diffuse field built from 12 HRTF directions on an imaginary cube do not carry over — the geometry and the FDN do, with the HRIR step replaced by the pan/delay/filter chain. And the published timings (a 0.73 s BRIR in 0.71 s on a 2.13 GHz Core 2 in MATLAB) are offline synthesis on 2010-era hardware, not a real-time budget; no measured CPU figures for a real-time ISM+FDN at 4–16 sources were found anywhere in this research.

Precedence-effect numbers bound the per-element delay stage, with the caveat that they come from a secondary summary naming but not reproducing the primary studies ([Wikipedia: Precedence effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedence_effect)): below **~2 ms** is summing localisation, where the image sits between the channels; **~2–5 ms** gives localisation dominance at the leading source; the precedence window extends to roughly **50 ms for speech** and **~100 ms for music**; and in the Haas window of about **10–30 ms** the delayed sound can be up to **10 dB louder** without reading as an echo. The usable band for width-without-echo on musical material is therefore roughly **1–30 ms**, and the sub-2 ms region that most "Haas widener" plugins use is also where the mono-sum combing is most destructive. Note the one gap the researchers flagged here: **no source gives a specific mono-safe inter-channel delay figure**, so no threshold should be published in the product's documentation as if it were established.

## Every convention puts pan on X and width on the glyph

Two families of display exist and should not be conflated. "Mix-as-space" editors treat the screen axes as *mix parameters the user drags*; analysers and object panners derive the picture *from* the signal or from object coordinates in physical units. The reader's stereo room is the first kind, and the second kind supplies the rendering techniques. Across the first family the axis assignments are consistent: **X is pan, near-universally**; the vertical axis is *either* level *or* frequency/tone but never both in a 2D view; depth is level or distance; and elevation gets a separate view or widget rather than a free third screen axis. iZotope's Visual Mixer is the plain case — drag nodes left or right for pan, up and down for output gain, with the axes not even explicitly labelled in the manual ([Neutron 4 Visual Mixer manual](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/neutron4/en/visual-mixer/index.html)). The three-axis case is Art DSP Canvas, whose vendor page states "**Left to right is pan… Up and down is tone.** Bass sits near the floor and air near the ceiling" and "**Front to back is volume** — Louder sounds come toward you" ([Art DSP Canvas](https://www.artdsp.net/canvas)). **The sources conflict here and the conflict is unresolved:** a review of the same product reports instead that "the X-axis controls panning, and the Y-axis controls volume and perceived depth" ([gearnews](https://www.gearnews.com/art-dsp-canvas-studio/)). The researcher's reconciliation — that in a perspective view screen-Y carries both height-as-tone and depth-as-volume, and the reviewer collapsed them — is **inference, not sourced**; treat the vendor page as authoritative and know that the ambiguity is real, because it is exactly the ambiguity a perspective stereo room will create for its own users.

Width is handled as a **property of the glyph**, never as an axis: Neutron edits it through "handles on its left and right edges" (and disables pan and width entirely for mono instances), while Canvas says "pull its edges to widen it" ([Neutron 4 manual](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/neutron4/en/visual-mixer/index.html); [Art DSP Canvas](https://www.artdsp.net/canvas)). That is a strong argument for a blob whose horizontal extent *is* the width parameter. Level is encoded three documented ways — as glyph size ("a white dot in the centre of each track graphic" that expands and contracts, [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/izotope-neutron-2-ozone-8)), as brightness ("Brighter sections indicate increased energy", [Halo Vision manual](https://nugenaudio.com/files/manuals/Halo%20Vision%20Manual.pdf)), or as a colour grade over the whole picture (Flux's "Power grading (modulated by overall RMS power)" and "Dynamic grading", [Flux Pure Analyzer vector scope](https://doc.flux.audio/analyzer/Vector_scope.html)). History is implemented as **per-frame decay plus progressive blur**, not as stored point lists: Flux's Fading is "the fade-to-black amount per frame" and its Blur kernel size is the "blur radius applied to past particles, so older particles smear more", with the warning that "values above 5 need a capable graphics card". That parameter set — per-frame decay, per-age blur, size relative to screen, colour driven by RMS or dynamics — is effectively a specification for a tasteful trail system, and exposing those as user controls is itself the convention. Three more conventions are cheap and worth copying: **dual ballistics** (a short thin fast line plus a thicker line with "inbuilt inertia"), **separate rise and fall time constants** ("Response" and "fall-back"), and a **Freeze button that pauses all views** ([Visualizer 2 manual](https://nugenaudio.com/files/manuals/Visualizer2%20Manual.pdf); [Halo Vision manual](https://nugenaudio.com/files/manuals/Halo%20Vision%20Manual.pdf)). Alerting should be threshold-triggered rather than constant decoration — correlation cells that "glow red when correlation drops below an adjustable threshold", a node meter that turns red on clipping.

What drives the picture is three documented mechanisms: level-driven progress along a path, band or tonal energy mapped to a spatial axis, and inter-channel correlation driving a per-pair graphic. The per-source pipeline that falls out (the researcher's assembly, not a published design) is a smoothed RMS or peak-with-decay for size, a short FFT or small filterbank for a **spectral centroid** on the tonal axis, and the three smoothed products L·R, L·L, R·R for correlation and width — a handful of MACs plus a one-pole each, so the cost is dominated by the FFT. Publish to the editor through a lock-free single-writer snapshot per source and never let `paint()` touch audio buffers. Two sourcing precisions matter here. The spectral centroid is defined in words as the **magnitude-weighted mean of bin frequencies**, with "a robust connection with the impression of brightness of a sound", and Grey and Gordon (1978) found the mean a better fit than the median variant ([Wikipedia: Spectral centroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_centroid)) — but the page's formula image did not survive retrieval, so `C = Σ f(n)·x(n) / Σ x(n)` is the standard expression of that stated definition, **not a quotation**; and computing it on a log frequency axis to match a log display is an inference, not something the sources state. Likewise the goniometer: every source describes it only in words as "a traditional X/Y plot rotated through 45 degrees" ([Visualizer 2 manual](https://nugenaudio.com/files/manuals/Visualizer2%20Manual.pdf); [RTW](https://www.rtw.com/en/blog/focus-the-audio-vectorscope.html)), and **no source found prints the X/Y equations** — so x = (L−R)/√2, y = (L+R)/√2 is an algebraic inference from the stated rotation, not a published formula. RTW's shape dictionary is the acceptance test for whatever you implement: dual mono at equal level → a vertical line, "the more vertical the pattern, the closer the mix is to mono"; one channel inverted → horizontal; left-only → a line rotated 45° left; a normal mix → a "ball of wool". Because a goniometer needs raw sample pairs while everything else needs about five floats per source per frame, prefer scalars for all sixteen elements and reserve a goniometer for the selected or soloed one.

On criticism, the honest finding is that **a sceptical-engineer literature was not located**. Searches for "eye candy" or "gimmick" critiques of these displays returned nothing relevant, and no usability study or A/B evidence on whether spatial mix displays improve mixing decisions was found. Reviewers are broadly positive but qualified: the Visual Mixer's graphical display is "rather neat" and a less intimidating entry point for newer mixers, the recorded complaint is that "Visual Mixer workflows could be better explained", and value is expertise-dependent — if you "know your way around a mix console already" you may "benefit less" ([Sound On Sound, Neutron 5](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/izotope-neutron-5)). The one substantive criticism of a spatial display found anywhere is the most useful finding in this section: Sound Particles' Energy Panner draws a trajectory that "describes only the plug-in's initial intentions", while the actual movement continues well past the drawn path "in a seemingly random way" ([Sound On Sound, Energy Panner](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/sound-particles-energy-panner)). The picture stopped being a truthful model of the processing, and that is what got called out. The design rule: **animate every blob from the actual current parameter and analysis values, never from the user's intended target or a precomputed path**, and if a path is drawn, draw the real current position as a distinct mark on top of it.

### Render sixteen blobs in 2D, because both GPU paths have failed in hosts

JUCE 8.0.15's renderer situation, read from the source tree rather than the marketing, is narrower than it appears. Windows exposes exactly two backends as a compile-time typelist — `contextDescriptorList<GDIRenderContext, D2DRenderContext>` in `modules/juce_gui_basics/native/juce_Windowing_windows.cpp`, with a source comment confirming "currently either GDI or Direct2D on Windows" — while the macOS peer offers only "Software Renderer" and "CoreGraphics Renderer" (`juce_NSViewComponentPeer_mac.mm`). **There is no selectable Metal renderer**: Metal appears only as the presentation layer beneath CoreGraphics, via `CoreGraphicsMetalLayerRenderer` drawing into a `CAMetalLayer`. `juce_opengl` still ships and is not deprecated — the module's only `[[deprecated]]` marker is on the legacy `OpenGLExtensions` helper. JUCE 8.0.0 added the Direct2D renderer and an animation module, and the changelog shows Direct2D needed sustained stabilisation across the whole 8.x line (fixes at 8.0.1, 8.0.2, 8.0.3, "many Direct2D bug fixes and performance improvements" at 8.0.7, more at 8.0.8, more at 8.0.10), alongside "Improved the performance of Component painting" at 8.0.13 and a minimum Windows target bumped to 1607.

Both GPU paths have documented multi-instance plug-in failures, which is the actual basis for the recommendation. With one `OpenGLContext` per instance on Windows, a developer reported the UI becoming "very unresponsive after a few instances", reproduced in Reaper and Mixbus but not standalone and not in hosts that run plugins in separate processes; **the resolution reported by the original poster was the swap interval** — the default is 1, and setting it to 0 fixed it with no visible tearing ([JUCE forum](https://forum.juce.com/t/openglrenderer-massive-performance-hit-with-multiple-instances/29913)). Separately, since JUCE 8.0.8, several open plugin UIs on Windows could freeze the host — windows kept animating and responded to hover while clicks registered nowhere — with JUCE developer reuk suspecting the message thread was spending all its time on paint commands and a user finding that the focused window was no longer prioritised, so background instances kept repainting under load ([JUCE forum](https://forum.juce.com/t/host-freezing-when-multiple-plugin-ui-instances-are-open-since-juce-8-0-8/67441)). A second patch that **removed the SwapChainThread** and fixed invalidated-region retrieval in `WM_PAINT` was reported to resolve it, submitted for review **16 December 2025**; the same thread reports the problem is not exclusive to Direct2D (OpenGL instances could also trigger it, it also occurred in 8.0.3, and after the patch "OpenGL is less affected… but it still lags"), and documents a shipping escape hatch of forcing the software renderer per instance with `getPeer()->setCurrentRenderingEngine(0)`. **Whether that fix is in a released build is an inference from changelog wording, not a confirmed mapping** — no released version is named in the thread, and the 8.0.10 and 8.0.13 entries may or may not be the same change. Treat the renderer as a hidden preference, not a fixed choice, since `getAvailableRenderingEngines()` and `setCurrentRenderingEngine()` exist on every peer.

For 4–16 translucent blobs, plain `Graphics`/`Path` with painter's-algorithm sorting is the lower-risk and entirely sufficient choice, and back-to-front ordering is a **correctness** requirement rather than an optimisation, because translucent blobs must composite in depth order. JUCE ships the 3D maths header-only and GL-free inside `juce_opengl/geometry/` — `Matrix3D` (which contains zero `#include` directives and provides `fromFrustum` and `rotation`), `Vector3D`, `Quaternion` and `Draggable3DOrientation`, the last being a ready-made drag-to-orbit helper that implies a quaternion camera and so avoids gimbal problems at the poles. Either add `juce_opengl` purely for those headers (licensing is per-framework, so under the commercial licence this changes nothing) or write ~60 lines of projection maths. Frame pacing should come from a **timestamped `VBlankAttachment`**, whose callback receives "a monotonically increasing value expressed in seconds that corresponds to the time of the next frame to be presented", explicitly intended for synchronising drawing across all classes using it; the older constructor is "only provided for compatibility". Given that the freeze was traced to background instances repainting, repaint discipline matters more than renderer choice: stop animating when the editor is not visible, and consider throttling when unfocused.

For the blobs themselves, the decisive JUCE 8 finding is **native, GPU-backed blur built into `Image`**: `applyGaussianBlurEffectInArea` is implemented via `CIGaussianBlur` on macOS (`juce_CoreGraphicsContext_mac.mm`) and via Direct2D on Windows (`juce_Direct2DImage_windows.cpp`), and JUCE's own `DropShadow` is now built on `applySingleChannelBoxBlurEffect`. That removes the historical reason to pull in a third-party stack blur — relevant because the licence of the Gin module the JUCE forum recommends was **not verified** in this research. Two caveats stand: the documentation warns that "the default implementation will modify the image pixel-by-pixel on the CPU, which will be slow", so it is only fast on native image types and will be slow under the Software Renderer; and `juce_Direct2DImage_windows.cpp` contains at least one `applyGaussianBlurEffectInArea` override whose body is `jassertfalse`, so **not every Direct2D-backed image supports native blur** — the exact conditions were not determined and must be tested on Windows. The recommended primitive is a **pre-rendered alpha-mask sprite tinted per source**: build one high-quality soft falloff once into an `Image` (via `Image::BitmapData` for exact control of the curve, which is the only route to an exact falloff such as the inverse-square the forum recommends or the classic `(1−r²)²` metaball weight), then per source per frame call `drawImageTransformed` with `fillAlphaChannelWithCurrentBrush = true`, documented as ignoring the image's RGB channels and filling with the current brush masked by alpha. Cost is one transformed blit per source, no `ColourGradient` is constructed at paint time — the documented cause of a reported 60→15 fps regression — per-source colour and width-as-x-scale fall out naturally, and it composes correctly with depth sorting. Metaball alpha-thresholding is the way to make blobs visibly *merge* (additive alpha, then zero everything below a cutoff), which is arguably the strongest expressive reason to choose blobs over dots, but the published algorithm's third step is a per-pixel read-modify-write of the whole surface each frame ([somethinghitme](https://somethinghitme.com/2012/06/06/2d-metaballs-with-canvas/)) — affordable at quarter resolution, not at full retina. Layered translucent ellipses remain the Software-Renderer fallback. Split the level mapping: **alpha and brightness for fast level changes, scale for slow ones**, which matches the documented dual-ballistic convention and avoids resizing every blob every frame. All of this performance ranking is inference from documented mechanism — draw-call count, per-pixel CPU work, native versus fallback paths. **No public measured benchmark of JUCE 8's renderers exists in this material**; the sources' own recommendation is to measure on your own hardware using the JUCE DemoRunner's graphics page, which shows rendering times for solid fills versus gradients across engines.

## Draw paths as parameters, and fix sixteen elements' IDs in version one

No shipping product solves depth with a clever 3D mouse gesture; they all sidestep it. dearVR PRO 2 switches among three orthographic views with a mode icon drawn in the pad ("X/Z is a top view", "Y/Z is a side view", "X/Y is a rear view"), zooms in **six fixed stages tied to physical units** (3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30 m), and in Polar mode **disables dragging entirely**, showing "A red position marker together with the note 'POLAR'" and moving elevation to its own slider ([dearVR PRO 2 manual](https://files.plugin-alliance.com/products/dearvr-pro-2/dearvr-pro-2_manual.pdf)). Canvas binds the third parameter to a different input channel — "scroll over an object to change its volume" — and supports marquee or Cmd-click multi-select with grouping, which is the feature that makes a display scale to many elements ([Art DSP Canvas](https://www.artdsp.net/canvas)). For a perspective room with 4–16 drum lanes, the scroll/modifier route is the only one of the three that needs no mode switch. Note two genuine category gaps: **snapping is undocumented in every manual surveyed** — no snap-to-centre, snap-to-speaker or quantised pan positions anywhere, the closest analogues being dearVR's discrete zoom stages and Dolby's step quantisation — and **per-source mute and solo are equally undocumented**; Neutron offers a hide (eye) button and renaming, Halo Vision's "lock" and label-filtering are display-scoped. So any snapping or solo design here is original work, which also means there is no convention to violate. One useful target for snapping does exist in mixing practice: Terry Manning's "Cardinal Points Law" starts mono sources at extreme left, centre or extreme right, leaving the space between for details that should stand out ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/making-most-stereo-panorama)). Keep solo, hide and lock as three visually distinct affordances, since they are a DSP action, a view action and an edit guard respectively.

Movement over bars has two established idioms and three storage models. Music auto-panners express movement as a tempo-synced, loopable drawn wave: Cableguys stores curve points with a three-state **weight** (Hard, Medium, Soft) plus a single global **Smooth** control rather than per-point Bezier handles, offers Pointer and three snapping Pens (Line, Arc, S-Curve), loops at triplet/straight/dotted values or bar lengths from **1/128 to 32 bars**, free-runs from 0.02 Hz to 5.24 kHz, and notably has **no numeric phase control** — you shift the wave by one grid space, or 1/16 of one with Shift ([Cableguys ShaperBox 3 Manual](https://www.cableguys.com/downloads/Cableguys-ShaperBox-3-Manual.pdf?v=3.3.1)). Its pan is a hybrid of level and Haas panning via a Pan/Haas slider, with Haas Range 0–40 ms (default about 2 ms) and a user-selectable pan law of 3, 4.5, 6 or 0 dB. Soundtoys' PanMan parameterises position in **degrees, not normalised −1…+1** — Offset ±105°, Width up to a 210° span, with yellow LEDs marking "beyond the speakers" — and expresses phase as groove ("Rush"/"Drag" via a Feel knob) rather than degrees ([Soundtoys PanMan Manual](https://www.fullcompass.com/common/files/33691-SoundToysPanManManual.pdf)); that manual contradicts itself on LFO range (0.1–10 Hz in one place, 0–20 Hz in another) and specifies no pan law. Spatial tools instead traverse a 2D/3D path over a musical duration: Dolby's is the best-documented trajectory editor, a sub-16-step tempo-synced sequencer where each step holds a path drawn with **Free draw, Line, Ellipse or Rectangle**, step duration **1/16 to 32 beats**, per-step direction with **arrowheads showing direction**, and a Linking control offering Copy, Mirror X, Mirror Y and Mirror XY for paired channels ([Using the Dolby Atmos Music Panner](https://www.hhb.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/Using-the-Dolby-Atmos-Music-Panner-Final.pdf)). The one **published, concrete trajectory data model** found stores no control points at all: the Trajectory Score Library defines trajectories as independent parametric functions — literally `@fun_def X($t, $r, $offsetX){$r *@cos($t)+$offsetX}` — sampled at a fixed grain (`@grain:=0.01`), traversed over a duration in **beats** with a loop count, with only circle, rectilinear and lissajou shapes implemented ([Babouri, JIM 2020](https://jim2020.sciencesconf.org/data/pages/BABOURI_N._Trajectory_Score_Library_1.pdf)). **No source documents a Bezier trajectory format in any audio product**, so Bezier should not be claimed as the industry norm.

The hybrid that fits this product is a **parametric shape per element as the host-automatable layer** (shape enum, radius/size, rate or division, phase, depth, centre x/y) with an optional drawn-path override stored as a non-parameter child `ValueTree` node under `apvts.state`, which is serialised by the existing `getStateInformation`/`setStateInformation` with `UndoManager` support and never touches the parameter count. The degenerate case of the parametric model is two independent LFOs, x = A_x·shape(f·t + φ_x), y = A_y·shape(f·t + φ_y), out of which circles (quarter-cycle phase offset, equal rates), figure-eights (2:1 rate ratio), lines (identical phase) and lissajous figures all fall for free — and it is per-element phase-offsettable by construction. Two decisions the sources do not make for you: traversing a drawn polyline by point index makes an element crawl through dense regions and leap across sparse ones, so **arc-length parameterisation** (cumulative chord length, look up by normalised distance) is needed for constant speed; and since no product examined exposes a phase knob, the cheapest way to stop sixteen elements moving in lockstep is a per-element phase in **0–100% of the loop period** plus a "spread phases evenly" button, with mirror-pair linking as a strong second feature because paired elements on mirrored paths keep the mix's energy centroid fixed by construction. Per-element **retrigger on note** matters as much as phase for an instrument: both Cableguys' MIDI/Audio trigger modes and PanMan's audio-triggered Random Step exist because clock-locked motion on a percussive source reads as unrelated wobble.

### The SmoothedValue ramp-restart pitfall, and equal power while moving

JUCE's house time constant for gain, pan and mix smoothing is **50 ms**, which appears as `reset (sampleRate, 0.05)` in `dsp::Panner`, `DryWetMixer`, `Chorus`, `Convolution` and `Oscillator`'s frequency smoother, against **1 ms** for a limiter's output stage. But `SmoothedValue` is a finite linear (or multiplicative) ramp whose `setTargetValue` **restarts the full ramp from the current value**, setting `countdown = stepsToTarget` and recomputing `step = (target − currentValue)/countdown` every call, so the ramp is always `stepsToTarget` samples long regardless of distance ([docs.juce.com SmoothedValue](https://docs.juce.com/master/classSmoothedValue.html)). At a 512-sample buffer (≈10.7 ms at 48 kHz) **a 50 ms ramp is re-aimed roughly five times before it ever completes**, producing a cascade of partial ramps — effectively a one-pole-ish 50 ms lag, which is exactly the "bouncing" chase pattern practitioners warn a one-pole produces under sparse automation ([KVR: General method for smooth parameter changes](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8638179)). For deliberate fast movement this is not academic: a 1/16-note pan flick at 128 BPM is about 117 ms, so a 50 ms lag eats a meaningful fraction of the gesture and audibly rounds off the extremes. The fix is to generate the movement signal at a known control rate and ramp over **exactly one control period** — `reset (controlBlockSamples)` with a 32- or 64-sample subdivision — so each ramp completes as the next target arrives, giving a continuous piecewise-linear curve with no lag and no overlap. This also matters because JUCE does not expose VST 3's sample-offset automation points to `processBlock` (the wrapper caches one value per block via `CachedParamValues`; this is the researcher's inference from the wrapper's structure and the absence of any per-sample API, not a documented statement), so **smoothing in the plugin is not polish — it is the only thing between host automation and zipper noise.** VST 3's own documentation states that the host transmits data allowing sample-accurate reconstruction and leaves the accuracy/cost trade to the plugin, and gives no guidance at all on zipper noise or interpolation ([VST 3 Parameters and Automation](https://steinbergmedia.github.io/vst3_dev_portal/pages/Technical+Documentation/Parameters+Automation/Index.html)).

Three more smoothing points are load-bearing. First, independently ramping L and R **does not preserve constant power during the ramp** — linear interpolation between two points on the unit circle cuts the chord, so instantaneous √(L²+R²) dips below unity mid-ramp. The dip is negligible for small per-block increments (the chord-versus-arc error scales with the square of the angle step) but grows with fast sweeps, and there are two clean fixes: smooth the **angle** and evaluate cos/sin per sample (exactly constant power; the trig cost of 1.5 M calls/second at 16 elements and 48 kHz is removed by a quarter-wave table or a recursive complex-multiply rotation), or keep JUCE's structure and **renormalise** by a cheap inverse square root per sample. Second, **`SmoothedValue<float, Multiplicative>` is the wrong tool for pan** — it is documented as never able to reach a target of zero and `jassert`s against it, while a pan gain must reach zero at the hard-panned extreme. Third, toggling an element's mono lock should `setTargetValue` to the centre gains so it glides, not `setCurrentAndTargetValue`, which snaps and clicks; the latter belongs only in `prepareToPlay`/`reset`. And one formula from that KVR thread **must not be reproduced**: a one-pole coefficient given as `1 - exp(-time/samplerate)` is dimensionally wrong — the exponent should be `-dt/tau`, i.e. `1 - exp(-1/(tau*fs))` — and the poster himself said he was working from memory. Note also that **no source gives a recommended pan-smoothing time constant in milliseconds**; the 50 ms and 1 ms figures are JUCE's own shipping choices read from source, which is strong evidence of convention but not published guidance, and no measured audibility threshold for a per-block gain step was found.

### Fixed banks, stable IDs, and the session contract

VST 3 permits up to **2³¹ parameters**, so the ceiling is host usability rather than the format — and the spec's binding rule is that **"IDs must never be reassigned, and the set of automatable parameters must not change through reconfiguration"**, with the warning that removing a parameter can lose automation data ([VST 3 Parameters and Automation](https://steinbergmedia.github.io/vst3_dev_portal/pages/Technical+Documentation/Parameters+Automation/Index.html)). That sentence decides the architecture: create parameters for the **maximum sixteen elements at construction, always**, even when the user is running four, and use an enable or active-count parameter to decide what the DSP and UI do — never to decide whether a parameter exists. A "selected element" bank that remaps breaks host automation in exactly the way the spec warns about, because a recorded lane's meaning would depend on hidden selection state. Sixteen elements with roughly ten movement parameters each lands near **160 parameters**, comfortably inside the practitioner guidance that "100s of parameters is fine, 1000s is questionable" and well under the ~600-parameter point at which APVTS *construction* (not processing) became the reported bottleneck, with startup around 20 seconds in debug and 5–6 seconds in release ([JUCE forum: Number of plugin parameters](https://forum.juce.com/t/number-of-plugin-parameters-any-limit/26204)). Host-side mitigations already exist — Pro Tools lets users choose which parameters are exported to automation tracks, and most hosts can automate the next parameter you touch — which is the practical reason to use `AudioProcessorParameterGroup` per element even though it "doesn't affect the functioning of individual parameters". **No numeric host-specific parameter limit was found for any DAW**; the frequently repeated "128 parameters" figure appears in forum titles but no authoritative source confirming it was obtained.

The ID discipline has three parts and one trap. Use zero-padded element indices (`elem01`…`elem16`, never `elem1`) with stable per-parameter suffixes, because JUCE hashes your string IDs into the host-facing `Vst::ParamID` via `VST3ClientExtensions::convertJuceParameterId` and into the AU `AudioUnitParameterID` via `hashCode()` with the sign bit cleared — **the string IDs are the saved-session contract**. Record a version hint per release, since `AudioProcessorParameter`'s documentation states that for AU ordering "you must ensure that the version hint supplied is a number higher than that of any parameter in any previous plugin version" ([docs.juce.com AudioProcessorParameter](https://docs.juce.com/master/classAudioProcessorParameter.html)). Group IDs need the same discipline plus one check: JUCE hashes them to VST 3 unit IDs with `& 0x7fffffff` and asserts if the result equals `Vst::kRootUnitId`, which fires only as a `jassert` in a Debug build — so test every shipped group ID in Debug. Three config flags silently change IDs and are documented with JUCE's own warnings: `JUCE_FORCE_USE_LEGACY_PARAM_IDS` ("Changing this setting may mess up user's DAW projects"), `JUCE_USE_STUDIO_ONE_COMPATIBLE_PARAMETERS` (default 1, and enabling it after a release changes IDs), and `JUCE_FORCE_LEGACY_PARAMETER_AUTOMATION_TYPE`. JUCE 8's sanctioned escape hatch is `VST3ClientExtensions::getCompatibleParameterIds()`, returning a map from old host-side ID to new JUCE string ID, with a guarantee worth designing around: **it is always called after `setStateInformation()`**, so a v2 with sixteen elements can read a version marker from its own state blob and return the right map for a v1 session ([docs.juce.com VST3ClientExtensions](https://docs.juce.com/master/classVST3ClientExtensions.html)). Build that version marker into the state from v1 even if it is never needed. Finally, cache the `std::atomic<float>*` from `getRawParameterValue` once per element in the constructor rather than doing a string lookup per block. One risk flagged but unverified: a forum thread titled "Parameter groups result in VST3 validator failures" was found in search results but not fetched, and the substantive caveats in the main `AudioProcessorParameterGroup` thread were truncated in retrieval — **validate groups against the VST 3 validator before shipping them.**

Mixing practice constrains the defaults more than the features. The consistent professional position is that **static placement is the default and movement is a feature**: "park them where they belong and move them only to make a feature", automated panning works well for spot effects, and "you can have too much of a good thing"; the named technique for movement without instability is a **subtle autopanned reverb return over a static dry source**; a mono check is a hard requirement for the rhythm section and vocals; and hard-panned reverb creates the greatest sense of space while large amounts can mask a source's direction, so a mono reverb panned to the same position as its source reinforces that position ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/making-most-stereo-panorama)). Practically: default the movement depth well below 100%, ship presets that move two or three elements rather than all sixteen, default the mono lock on for the lowest-pitched element, and put a mono-sum audition button next to any Haas control. The "gimmicky" failure mode has a specific technical signature — all elements sharing one rate and phase, which collapses per-element panning into a single global width wobble — and a second one, movement fast enough to read as tremolo rather than travel, though **no source quantifies the rate at which that transition happens**, so that threshold is principle rather than citation. A per-element left/right energy balance readout is implied by the guidance to keep the mix's left and right energy roughly balanced, which with sixteen independently travelling elements is easy to lose.

Two structural notes follow. "We support SOFA loading" and "we ship an HRTF set" are **completely different licence problems**: supporting user files via libmysofa costs nothing legally, while bundling a dataset requires per-dataset clearance — which is why bundling MIT KEMAR with citation and letting users load their own SOFA files is the clean combination. And reading GPL-family plugin source for its algorithm choices and then writing your own implementation of the published algorithm is normal practice; copying its code is not, and for IEM in particular that line matters. Everything in the recommended build — pan law, side high-pass, per-element delay and tilt, order-1 early reflections, one shared FDN — can be written with **no dependency at all**, which is the cheapest possible licence position.

## Conclusion

The research inverts the apparent difficulty of this feature. The DSP is nearly trivial — a few multiplies per element, one biquad on the Side path, one shared reverb bus — and the hard parts are a UI problem and a compatibility problem. The UI problem has one rule that matters more than any aesthetic choice: the only spatial display that drew published criticism was the one whose picture diverged from what its processing actually did, so every blob must be animated from measured state, and a drawn path must carry the real current position as a separate mark. The compatibility problem is that **the string parameter IDs are the session contract**, hashed into VST 3 and AU identifiers the host saves, and VST 3 forbids the parameter set changing through reconfiguration — so the single most consequential decision in the build happens before any audio flows: sixteen element groups with zero-padded IDs, version hints recorded per release, a version marker in the state blob, and `getCompatibleParameterIds` kept in reserve. Get that wrong and users' projects break in a way no amount of DSP polish repairs.

Three things are worth verifying on the reader's own machine before committing, because the research could not settle them. Measure the renderers with the JUCE DemoRunner's graphics page and test native blur on a Direct2D-backed image, since **no public benchmark of JUCE 8's renderers exists** and at least one Direct2D blur override is a `jassertfalse`; keep the renderer switchable and keep the "force software renderer" escape hatch in the code, because the multi-instance freeze fix was still pending review on 16 December 2025 and its presence in any released build is an inference from changelog wording, not a confirmed fact. Check the four unverified licences (Gin, chowdsp_utils, `oximedia-metering`, and the `saf_tracker` state of any SAF build's CMake configuration) before a line of their code enters the tree. And if any Steam Audio-derived air-absorption coefficient is used, verify the third band against upstream source rather than copying the **0.182** that reads as −158 dB at 100 m. The deeper point about distance is worth building into the product's language rather than its marketing: measured air absorption at mix-scale distances is about a decibel, so the "3D-sounding" result the reader wants comes almost entirely from direct-to-reverberant ratio, a handful of early reflections, and the precedence effect — the physics is a seasoning, and the published room model that listeners could not distinguish from measured reality replaced the whole of air absorption with one fixed 14 kHz corner.

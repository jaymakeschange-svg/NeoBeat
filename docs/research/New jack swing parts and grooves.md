# Rebuilding new jack swing from structure alone

New jack swing has no documented numeric specification, but the machine that produced it does — and that asymmetry is the key to writing the style with entirely different sounds. The swing mechanism is primary-sourced and exact: the Akai/Linn percentage is the share of each eighth-note pair given to the *first* sixteenth, it runs from 50% (straight) to 75% (3:1), and **it only ever delays the even-numbered sixteenths** ([Akai MPC3000 Operator's Manual, p.63](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/207365/Akai-Mpc-3000.html?page=63)). Everything above that mechanism is thinner than its reputation. There is **no documented tempo range for new jack swing anywhere in the literature** — not Wikipedia, not Britannica, not the one published recreation tutorial, which states no BPM at all ([Sweetwater](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/recreating-80s-new-jack-swing-in-the-box/)) — so this report supplies none. There is no microtiming study of new jack swing records, no published transcription set of its drum parts, and effectively no scholarly work on its harmony. What *is* programmable comes from four directions: the exact swing arithmetic; one quantitative study of a neighbouring repertoire (30 sampled drum breaks, median swing **1.2:1 ≈ 54.5%**, uncorrelated with tempo, snare late on beat 2 but not beat 4) ([*Music Perception* 34(3):291](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1525/mp.2017.34.3.291)); producer testimony about role allocation, selective quantising and register; and hard sampler memory ceilings that dictate envelope shapes independent of timbre. For the house and techno presets the picture inverts — the patterns are well specified by craft convention but have **no acoustic measurement behind them at all**, and two mutually incompatible swing scales circulate in those sources, which is the one error in this report that would ruin a preset bank outright.

## Swing is one number on one note value — and two different scales claim it

The mechanism is unambiguous and worth implementing literally. On the MPC3000 the Swing% field **exists only for 1/16 and 1/8 note values**, the manual frames swing as "a variation of timing correction," 50% is even, 66% makes the first note twice as long as the second (a "technically perfect swing," triplets with a silent middle note), and 75% makes it three times as long, "a very exaggerated swing" ([Akai MPC3000 manual, p.63](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/207365/Akai-Mpc-3000.html?page=63)). Roger Linn's own account confirms the implementation and its origin: swing came from delaying "alternate 16th notes" during playback, he named it **"shuffle" in 1979**, and it was a RAM-saving accident — early prototypes stored only sixteenths at one byte each, so delaying alternate sixteenths let him store swung beats compactly ([Attack Magazine, Roger Linn interview](https://attackmagazine.com/features/interview/roger-linn-swing-groove-magic-mpc-timing/2)). At the LM-1's **48 parts per quarter note** he lists usable values of **50, 54, 58, 60, 62, 66, 70 and 75%** and says he rarely needs more increments ([Attack](https://attackmagazine.com/features/interview/roger-linn-swing-groove-magic-mpc-timing/2)). Seven of those eight are exactly the integer tick positions at 48 ppq (delaying the even sixteenth to tick 12–18 yields 50.00, 54.17, 58.33, 62.50, 66.67, 70.83, 75.00) — **the 60% does not fit the series and is probably a recollection slip**, so treat it as non-canonical. That derivation is the researcher's arithmetic, not a sourced claim, as is the millisecond formula that follows from it: delay = 300 × (P − 50) / BPM. The tempo columns below are illustrative values for reading the formula, chosen to span a plausible working range — they are not a claim about the genre's tempo, which no source states.

| Swing % | 95 BPM (16th = 158 ms) | 110 BPM (16th = 136 ms) | 120 BPM (16th = 125 ms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 (straight) | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 54 | 12.6 ms | 10.9 ms | 10.0 ms |
| 54.5 (= 1.2:1, measured break median) | 14.2 ms | 12.3 ms | 11.3 ms |
| 58 | 25.3 ms | 21.8 ms | 20.0 ms |
| 62 | 37.9 ms | 32.7 ms | 30.0 ms |
| 66.7 (triplet, 2:1) | 52.6 ms | 45.5 ms | 41.7 ms |
| 75 (3:1) | 78.9 ms | 68.2 ms | 62.5 ms |

The only quantitative measurement in this whole subject area is the break study, and its scope has to be stated precisely because it is tempting to over-read. It measured **30 classic drum breaks from hip-hop's breakbeat canon** — not new jack swing records — and found sixteenth-note swing, defined as a systematic delay of the even-numbered sixteenth divisions, common across the sample, with a **median ratio of 1.2:1**, swing magnitude **uncorrelated with tempo**, the snare **frequently delayed on beat 2 but not on beat 4**, and it introduced "swing density" (how often the even divisions actually contain events) as a factor in how much swing is perceived ([*Music Perception* 34(3):291](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1525/mp.2017.34.3.291)). Converting by P = R/(R+1), 1.2:1 is **54.5%** — between the two lowest Linn detents, in the barely-swung zone rather than the shuffle zone. This is the sampled-break repertoire that fed this music, which makes it the best available proxy and still only a proxy. The tempo-independence finding has a direct design consequence: do not derive swing depth from tempo. And because the millisecond offset shrinks as tempo rises while the perceptual threshold does not, holding a constant percentage across a tempo change makes a groove feel progressively straighter — Linn's remark that "certain swing settings work at some tempos but not at others" is arithmetically expected ([Attack, p.3](https://www.attackmagazine.com/features/interview/roger-linn-swing-groove-magic-mpc-timing/3/)).

The scale hazard is real and unresolved in the sources. Two conventions circulate, and conflating them produces presets that are wrong by tens of milliseconds.

| Convention | Straight | Triplet shuffle | Seen in |
|---|---|---|---|
| MPC / Linn / Logic | **50%** | 66.7% | Akai manual; Linn; Attack's *Beat Dissected* recipes |
| Cubase / FL / "groove amount" | **0%** | 100% | FL, Cubase; the Ableton informant in Danielsen et al. |

Attack states "0% in FL/Cubase equates to 50% in Logic/MPC. 100% in FL = 66.6% in Logic," and explicitly says converting is *not* a simple add-or-subtract-50; a reader challenged the article's conversion factor and the page never resolved it ([Attack, "DAW & drum machine swing"](https://attackmagazine.com/technique/passing-notes/daw-drum-machine-swing/4)). Ableton mixes both, with 50%-is-straight and 0%-is-straight behaviour depending on the groove chosen, and negative swing (below 50% on the MPC scale) moves the weak sixteenth *earlier* for a rushed feel ([Attack, "Going off grid"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/tutorials/going-off-grid-what-is-swing-and-how-to-add-it/)). Label every swing figure in NeoBeat with its scale, and pick one internally. Two further cautions: there is **no documented swing or shuffle function on the E-mu SP-1200 at all** — the one secondary source giving its tick resolution misstates the LM-1's in the same sentence, so any specific SP-1200 swing percentage you meet should be treated as unsourced; and no source gives the MPC60 or MPC3000 internal tick resolution, so the famous "MPC swing" detents are properly the **LM-1 grid Linn carried forward**, not a documented MPC60 spec ([Attack](https://attackmagazine.com/features/interview/roger-linn-swing-groove-magic-mpc-timing/2)).

## The drums and the stabs are two different questions

The most-quoted technical definition of the style — "a rhythmic pattern using offbeat accented 16th note triplets" — should not be used as a definition. Wikipedia attributes it to a 2017 Revolt oral-history feature, and when the researcher fetched that article it **did not define "swingbeat" and did not mention sixteenth-note triplets anywhere**; the closest statement in it is Kelly Griffin's "it had a shuffle to it," with Griffin adding that the swing was *less defined* than on Run-DMC's "My Adidas" ([Revolt](https://www.revolt.tv/article/2017-10-23/45416/groove-me-the-story-of-new-jack-swing/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_jack_swing)). So the citation chain is broken, and the phrase is also musicologically muddled: swung sixteenths and sixteenth-note triplets are different grids, and "offbeat accented 16th-note triplets" specifies no ratio.

Set against that, Riley in his own RBMA lecture describes something quite specific — he rejected quantisation (he called the Korg DDD-1 "perfect" because it had "No quantization"), he often played parts live straight to tape, he says sequencers could not capture the off-and-on swing he wanted, and on the MPC he built **custom swing templates he describes as a mix of "16 triplets and then one 16"** ([Teddy Riley, RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)). Read carefully, the apparent contradiction largely dissolves once the lanes are separated. The triplet-grid talk belongs to the **stab and hit layer**, where Riley was hand-building a non-uniform template; the sixteenth-note swing arithmetic belongs to the **kick/snare/hat layer**, where the machine's global percentage acted. That reading is corroborated from the other side of the glass: Prince Charles Alexander describes trying to reverse-engineer which swing setting on the MPC's sixteenth-note patterns Riley was using — i.e. a single, identifiable, non-obvious percentage governed the sixteenth patterns ([RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)) — and he was still guessing. The programmable version, and this is inference rather than cited fact, is a two-grid system: a global even-sixteenth swing for the kit, plus a separate stab lane whose offsets are *per-step and non-uniform*, mixing triplet-grid positions with straight sixteenths inside one bar. If you build only one of the two, build the second; the uniform lean is the part a stock swing knob already gives you.

Two placement rules are concrete enough to encode without genre-specific transcription, and they agree across sources. First, **the swung displacement lives at the tail of the bar or phrase**: the one published recreation puts "a swung kick at the end of each bar" across an eight-bar phrase, with a pronounced triplet fill as the phrase turnaround and a straighter pattern with a single swung kick in intro and outro ([Sweetwater](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/?p=232776)), and the same-period hip-hop template independently specifies a swung kick on the last sixteenth of beat 1 and a **swung snare on the last sixteenth of the two-bar loop**, both with snap-to-grid off ([Native Instruments](https://www.native-instruments.com/blogs/blog/drum-patterns)). Keep the downbeat kicks on-grid and put the displacement on the late-bar hit, where it functions as a pickup rather than as part of the core pulse. Second, **the backbeat snare is a separate timing object from the swing grid**. The break study found delay on beat 2 but not beat 4 ([*Music Perception*](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1525/mp.2017.34.3.291)), and four independently transcribed modern grooves found the snare *early* while hats, bass or synth carried the swing ([The Pudding, "Groove"](https://pudding.cool/2023/06/groove/)). Nudge beat 2 late, leave beat 4 on the grid, and if anything pull the snare forward rather than letting global swing carry it back. The widely repeated claim that new jack swing snares are *displaced off* 2 and 4 is unverified — every placement source found, across funk, hip-hop, rock and drum'n'bass, puts the snare on 2 and 4 with the swung snare as an *additional* late hit.

"Swing density" explains why the hat lane is decisive, and it is the most useful single idea here for a sequencer designer. Since swing only displaces notes that fall on even sixteenths, **the part playing the most even-sixteenth events is the part that audibly carries the swing**: a continuous sixteenth hat line exposes the setting fully, while a kick and snare sitting on downbeats and eighths expose almost none of it. That is the structural reason a producer can set one global swing and still get "swung hats over a square kick," and it is directly attested as deliberate practice on the MPC60 — Scotty Hard said kick and snare could land on the beat while hi-hats were swung, calling it one of the machine's best features ([RBMA](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/11/mpc-instrumental-instruments)). Documented per-part splits run both directions: heavier swing on kick and snare with lighter swing on hi-hat on "Chameleon," and consistently late offbeat sixteenth hats on "Superstition" ([Ethan Hein, "Swing primer"](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2021/swing-primer/)); hat at quintuplet 60% with kick straight and snare early, or synth and hat swung with kick and snare straight ([The Pudding](https://pudding.cool/2023/06/groove/)). **All of that mixed-swing evidence is from adjacent repertoire — Dilla-era and 1970s funk — not from new jack swing records**, where nobody has measured bass or keyboard timing at all. The historically plausible structure for this genre is not "swung drums versus deliberately straight bass" but uniformly swung machine parts with hand-played parts running their own, usually lighter, ratio; that is inference. A matching parameter set, also inference: one global swing percentage, a per-part swing *scaling* from 0 to 100% of global, and a separate per-part static offset in milliseconds that can go negative for the snare. Two period details are worth having in the fill generator: flams in shuffle programming were written as **32nd-note triplets** — roughly 45 ms ahead of the main hit at 110 BPM, far wider than modern 10–20 ms layering flams — with 64th-triplet flams on side stick and 32nd-triplet double flams on hat at the start of each bar, and repeated offbeat side-stick figures at the end of each two-bar phrase ([*Music Technology*, Dec 1989](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/on-the-beat/196)). And placing hits on a triplet grid then *deleting* notes is a distinct generative method from percentage swing: it locks displacements to exact 66.7% positions with gaps, rather than applying a uniform lean ([*Music Technology*](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/on-the-beat/196)).

## Registers, voicings and cell lengths survive the timbre swap

The harmonic side has the thinnest evidence of anything here: there is **no peer-reviewed, transcription-based study of new jack swing harmony**, no published voicing transcriptions, and the specialist literature is one book cited only at second hand. What survives are claims that appear independently in a producer account *and* in an analysis of a record. The provenance is gospel keyboard transplanted onto hip-hop drums: Keith Sweat's brief for "I Want Her" was to take "those church chords" and combine them with the beats Riley was making for rappers, Riley played piano in church from about age nine, and he wanted "that church feel" specifically to keep people on the dancefloor — a dance device, not a ballad device — citing Manhattan Transfer, Take 6 and the Temptations, i.e. vocal-group harmony rather than instrumental jazz ([SongwriterUniverse](https://www.songwriteruniverse.com/teddy-riley-songwriter-producer-michael-jackson-guy/); [RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)). The two available chord analyses of period hits both come out as **three-chord loops of extended diatonic chords with no secondary dominants and no modulation**: "Poison" in F# minor, 4/4, with a pre-chorus of **iv9 – v7 – i7** (note the minor v7, i.e. Aeolian rather than harmonic minor in the keys) ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/bell-biv-devoe/poison)), and "I Wanna Sex You Up" in D major with a verse of **I9 – ii7 – V11** ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/color-me-badd/i-wanna-sex-you-up)). Both are crowd-sourced and only partially entered, and their tempo fields are unreliable enough to ignore — the same database lists Keith Sweat's "Make It Last Forever" at 168, i.e. counted double-time ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/artist/keith-sweat)). The transferable rule: pick two to four diatonic chords, make every one a 7th or 9th, loop them, and get richness from extensions rather than root motion. A tutorial claim that the style favours Eb, F and Gb major "for vocal brightness" conflicts with both analysed hits and with Hooktheory's two minor-key Keith Sweat entries, and should be discarded ([Melodigging](https://www.melodigging.com/genre/new-jack-swing)).

Voicing and placement guidance exists only in generic form, so treat it as a sensible default rather than a documented period practice: build on minor-7 voicings stated as **3rd–5th–b7 with the root omitted because the bass covers it**, keep stabs *off* the main drum hits and on offbeat sixteenths, mix short hits with a few sustained ones for internal call-and-response, add the 11th adjacent to chord tones for a darker cluster, place the 9th in a different octave from the rest, and change one chord late in the loop to make the progression catchier ([Attack, "Levelling Up Your Chord Stabs"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/passing-notes/levelling-up-your-chord-stabs/)). The register plan that falls out of the producer testimony is a three-band division — bass fused with the kick at the bottom and deliberately obscured, a rootless mid-register cluster for stabs, stacked vocals above, pads in the extreme top — and it is inference from Riley's statements rather than a measured finding. Those statements are quotable, though: he **deliberately buried the low end under the kick so the bass is felt rather than heard**, describing the result as what you hear "through the walls"; kick and bass were **tuned to work together** as a unit; snares and claps were deliberately loud; and he held drums and bass back so the vocal could sit on top ([RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)).

The bass itself is the weakest-evidenced of the core parts, and the one published transcription is contested in its own comments. Documented: the signature sound is a factory 4-operator FM preset Riley calls the "Lately bass," reused constantly, which he played from a **Yamaha DX100 — a keyboard of about 25 keys** ([RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/); [RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)). The patch naming is contested: a detailed archive puts the original Lately Bass on the **Yamaha TX81Z**, with the DX100's similar patch being "Solid Bass" and "a bit different," portable across 4-op machines including the DX21, DX27, DX11, YS200 and V50 but not the DX9 or FB01, and built roughly on the second FM algorithm with operators 1, 2 and 4 on sine and operator 3 on wave 5 ([Polynominal](https://www.polynominal.com/lately-bass-yamaha-tx81z-dx100/index.html)). On "My Prerogative," an analysis reads the line as **A harmonic minor using the raised 7th (G#)**, with half-step tension and resolution around that note supplying the character — but a reader argues the first three notes of bar 2 (G, Ab, B) spell b3–3–5 of an **E7#9**, implying a transcription error, and the dispute is unresolved ([No Treble](https://www.notreble.com/buzz/2016/10/07/talking-style-new-jack-swing/)). What both readings share is more useful than which is right: a **chromatic half-step approach into the root or third inside an otherwise minor-pentatonic cell**. Player transcriptions of "Poison" show the same shape — a narrow oscillation (F#–E–F#–E) with notes added or repeated to fit the beat, two parts ending G#→A and A→G# ([TalkBass](https://www.talkbass.com/threads/bell-biv-devoe-poison.597965/)). The transferable constraint, part cited and part inference: **1–2 bar riff cells from a tiny pitch set, roughly an octave and a half of range, short notes, looped with small rhythmic variation rather than through-composed**. No source states registers, octaves, note lengths, or how the bass times against the kick, and no source settles whether it doubles the kick or plays into its gaps — do not let a preset assert any of that as fact. Riley also documents the opposite move: on one of his best-known records the team tried to put bass in and concluded "it just doesn't work," and left it out ([MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/artists/you-just-cant-put-bass-on-a-song-like-this-what-would-you-put-we-tried-to-have-bass-in-there-it-just-doesnt-work-teddy-riley-on-how-he-took-a-single-bar-of-a-classic-bill-withers-song-and-turned-it-into-one-of-the-greatest-r-and-b-records-of-all-time)).

Three more structural units are documented well enough to build. The **"tag"** is Riley's own term for a short recurring instrumental phrase whose job is to keep the beat from stopping, and he says "melody is king" — melodies written first, lyrics over finished melodies, starting from hummed ideas recorded on a phone and beat-detected into time ([RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)). Melodic ranges in the two analysed tracks are narrow — A3–F#4 and A3–B4, about a minor 6th and 14 semitones ([Hooktheory: Poison](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/bell-biv-devoe/poison); [I Wanna Sex You Up](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/color-me-badd/i-wanna-sex-you-up)), so tags and counter-melodies should stay inside about an octave and avoid wide leaps. The **sustained layer is an identity timbre, not a harmonic voice**: the Ensoniq Mirage sound that opens "Miss You Much" was the only sound carried over from *Control* and recurs on "Escapade" and "Love Will Never Do," the chorus's high string patch was a single note Janet Jackson was counted in to play, and on "Rhythm Nation" a **two-bar guitar loop** from Sly & The Family Stone's "Thank You" was the harmonic foundation with LinnDrum over it and the string line added last ([RBMA Key Tracks](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2014/09/jimmy-jam-interview)). And the **"stop and drop"** is the genre's breakdown mechanism: Riley built songs around breaks while one groove element — he names the **shaker** — kept dancers moving, with the pre-chorus as his main hook device and a deliberate final element to keep energy going at the end ([RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)). In a transferred palette the surviving element should be whatever occupies the shaker's role, a dry high sixteenth-rate texture, not a pad or a chord.

Two disputes to carry rather than resolve. **Horns are contested between sources**: one strand treats "big horns and big drums" as hallmarks of the late-80s/early-90s R&B of L.A. Reid and Babyface, who produced most of Bobby Brown's *Don't Be Cruel* ([Music Sermon](https://medium.com/musicsermon/the-new-jack-swing-testament-b2378a914816)), while a participant in Riley's circle presents jazz horns and saxophones as a **later** arrival, entering as the style faded ([RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)). The likely explanation is a producer-camp difference, not a factual dispute, so treat horn stabs as optional and camp-specific rather than a genre marker; the only primary-sourced horn role in the Riley camp is a **solo melodic voice** carrying the emotional peak (his keyboard, saxophone and harmonica "signature solos"), not section punctuation ([RBMA lecture](https://www.redbullmusicacademy.com/lectures/teddy-riley-lecture/)). The orchestra hit, meanwhile, has a traceable lineage — ORCH5 was an **8-bit sample of the *Firebird Suite* chord opening the "Infernal Dance," pitched down a minor sixth**, and the sound was still on records inside the window, e.g. En Vogue's "Hold On" (1990) ([Wikipedia, Orchestra hit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestra_hit)) — but **no source documents ORCH5 or any specific hit sample on a Riley, Guy, Bobby Brown, Keith Sweat or BBD record**, and the widely circulated Synclavier association is not supported by the orchestra-hit source, which does not mention the Synclavier at all. What does transfer is the function: a single-event, wide-bandwidth, sforzando-envelope punctuation placed where a drum fill would go, at phrase boundaries and anticipations rather than on downbeats. And because Riley's own stabs were often **himself jamming riffs, sampled and chopped into "twisted" one-shots** for "Remember The Time" ([MusicRadar](https://www.musicradar.com/news/michael-jackson-dangerous-teddy-riley-quincy-jones)), many stabs in this idiom are performed phrases reduced to one-shots — which is why they sit slightly off-grid and carry player articulation, and why slicing a played phrase reproduces the role better than sequencing a synth chord.

## Memory ceilings, 20 milliseconds and selective quantising did the sound design

Three findings here are worth more to a sample-based reconstruction than any timbre description, because they are envelope and workflow rules that any source material can satisfy. The first is the **memory ceiling**. The SP-1200 held **10 seconds of sampling total with individual sounds limited to 2.5 seconds** at 12-bit/26.04 kHz, and the documented working method around that was chopping samples into shorter pieces and rearranging them, plus sampling sped-up sources and pitching them back down ([Wikipedia, SP-1200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mu_SP-1200)); the MPC60 held **13.1 seconds of mono audio** at 12-bit/40 kHz, or 26.2 with expansion ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/music-business/akai-mpc60-revisited)), and producers sped records to **78 RPM** to fit more material, then slowed them down in the machine ([RBMA](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/11/mpc-instrumental-instruments)). The ceiling was an economic accident, not an aesthetic choice — Dave Rossum's engine could address 20 seconds in 1984, but RAM prices made it prohibitive ([Rossum Electro-Music](https://www.rossum-electro.com/pages/education-sp-1200)). The consequence for Batchcut and NeoBeat is a transferable envelope rule: **one-shots whose decay ends because memory ended rather than because the envelope decayed** — an abrupt amplitude discontinuity mid-decay — with no room for reverb tails inside the sample, so ambience is added downstream. A second reproducible artefact is the spectral tilt and aliasing from drop-sample pitch shifting, which was considered musically useful rather than a defect, along with the brightness added by the absence of a reconstruction filter ([Wikipedia, SP-1200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mu_SP-1200)). Worth noting for layering: the SP-1200's filtering is **asymmetric by output pair** — channels 1–2 have dynamic SSM2044 filters, 3–4 a lower fixed cutoff, 5–6 a higher fixed cutoff, 7–8 no filter at all ([Rossum](https://www.rossum-electro.com/pages/education-sp-1200)) — so the same sample came back with different brightness depending on where it was assigned. Reproducing that structurally means building three or four brightness variants of each one-shot and distributing a pattern across them, rather than one static sound. And the 12-bit rates imply usable bandwidth ceilings around 13 kHz and 20 kHz, so brightness in this music was carried by **transient edge and aliasing imaging, not high-frequency extension** — an inference, but a directly actionable one for your own material.

The second finding is the **20-to-30 millisecond attack point**. The D-50's designer Tadao Kikumoto says about **90% of a sound's identity comes from the overtone changes in the first 20 to 30 milliseconds**, which is why the instrument used granular PCM for the initial portion, aiming for a sound "close to real, but obscure" ([Roland D-50 30th anniversary](https://www.roland.com/uk/promos/d-50_30th_anniversary/)). For a preset family built on substituted sonics, that is the single most useful design statement in this research: identity lives in roughly the first 1/32 note, so **preset variants should differ in their first 20–30 ms while sharing the sustain body**. It also pairs with a hardware fact that explains the era's timbral logic — the M1's filter is non-resonant and the S950's VCF is static with no resonance control ([Wikipedia, Korg M1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korg_M1); [muzines S950 review](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/akai-s950/1372)) — so contrast between parts came almost entirely from sample choice and layering, not filter movement.

The third is the **quantise policy**, and it is the clearest documented statement of how these grooves were actually shaped. On *Rhythm Nation 1814*, Jam and Lewis played parts live rather than sequencing them; **beats were quantized but fills and turnarounds were not**, alternate patterns were triggered on the fly, and cymbals and other percussion were overdubbed live just before mixing ([Reverb](https://reverb.com/news/the-making-of-janet-jacksons-rhythm-nation)). Jam is blunter still: "I never sequenced any of the keyboards," with the Oberheim OB-8 as his main writing instrument, and "nothing was sequenced" — sections were switched by hand on tape, to the point that remix engineers asked for section start times the producers could not supply ([RBMA Key Tracks](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2014/09/jimmy-jam-interview)). The Riley camp arrives at the same place by a different route: the first Guy album was largely recorded live with players performing every part and much of it improvised, edited with tape and razor blades before sequencers were in use ([RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)). Combine that with the MPC60's hard sixteenth-note autocorrect — notes are stored in the nearest sixteenth slot, which Linn decided "was not a bug but rather a feature" ([RBMA](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/11/mpc-instrumental-instruments)) — and the rule is: **quantise the load-bearing low and mid hits, leave the subdivision layer, the fills and the keys unquantised**. The implication for preset architecture is that the style's life sits in **pattern substitution at bar and phrase boundaries** and in unquantised fills, not in micro-timing of the backbeat. Ship several pattern variants and a separate fill articulation rather than one humanised loop; a copy-pasted MIDI loop will miss the idiom even with the right notes. Two final timbral relations worth encoding because they are relational rather than sonic, and so survive any substitution: the snare was **loud, abrasive and deliberately out of tune with the rest of the track**, which Hank Shocklee names as a defining trait and contrasts with later pop that pulled the snare back and tuned it to the track ([RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)); and arrangements were deliberately **sparse**, in explicit contrast to the lush multi-part 1980s productions that preceded them, with simple melodies and the drums in the foreground ([RBMA oral history](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/)). One absence to respect: **no source documents a gate or compressor model, threshold, ratio or hold time for new jack swing drums**. The genre's reputation for heavy gating is widely asserted and has no primary engineering account behind it.

## House and techno invert which lane carries the motion

The house and techno side is the mirror image of the above: the patterns are specified in satisfying numeric detail, but almost all of that detail comes from one magazine's editorial recipes, and **no acoustic-measurement study of house or techno microtiming exists** with published millisecond deviations or swing ratios. The most-cited candidate fails on inspection — Danielsen et al., "Shaping rhythm: timing and sound in five groove-based genres," *Popular Music* 42(1), 2023, is despite its title a **qualitative interview study**: 25 musicians and producers interviewed in 2017–2018, typically 60–90 minutes each, coded by genre and topic, reporting **no measured millisecond deviations or swing ratios**, with all its quantities coming from informants' statements ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/shaping-rhythm-timing-and-sound-in-five-groovebased-genres/BBC410F9849DB982AEBFACEA14D38F32)). One of its figures should be discarded outright: an informant describing chopping samples "perhaps half a second" early for a laid-back feel is loose speech, since half a second at 120 BPM is a full beat.

What the sources do agree on is the invariant. Four-on-the-floor plus **an open hi-hat on every second eighth note** is the canonical pattern, inherited from disco, and Julien and Levaux describe the kick as a pulse "regular like the piston of a mechanical machine" with the open hat providing "a sense of pull and push in between the beats" ([Techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)). That function explains why the open hat is non-negotiable: it is the sole counterweight to an invariant kick, and on a 1-indexed 16-step bar it sits on **steps 3, 7, 11 and 15**. It is also the highest-value single toggle for distinguishing these presets from your existing library, because the offbeat eighth is structurally unoccupied in new jack swing — nothing there reserves the "and" of every beat for a single marked, loud, long-decay event.

Tempo cannot be the discriminator. Attack's *Beat Dissected* recipes specify **120–125 BPM for all four classic styles** — 80s Chicago house, Motor City Detroit techno, Belleville techno and 90s Jersey garage house — while Wikipedia gives house 115–130 with 120–130 most typical, techno 120–150, and minimal techno an average of 125–130 ([House music, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_music); [Techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno); [Minimal techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_techno)). Since your own working floor for the new jack swing material is 118 BPM, a 120–125 house or techno preset sits at *the same tempo* as much of the existing library — so the contrast has to be carried entirely by grid, hat behaviour and backbeat treatment. Note also that **deep house, garage house, Chicago house and acid house have no sourced BPM range** on Wikipedia's styles page; the figures that circulate for them come from commercial BPM-chart sites citing nothing ([Styles of house music, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styles_of_house_music)).

Sources for the table rows: [80s-Style Chicago House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/80s-style-chicago-house/); [Motor City Detroit Techno](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/motor-city-detroit-techno/); [Belleville Techno](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/belleville-techno/); [90s Jersey Garage House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/90s-jersey-garage-house/); [Tracksensei](https://tracksensei.com/blog/how-to-arrange-a-techno-track). **Every swing figure in that table is on the MPC scale where 50% is straight**, and all of them are editorial recipes rather than measurements. The one producer-reported figure in the literature uses the *other* scale: an EDM producer described a swing "pocket" of roughly **22–40% in Ableton Live, where 0 is straight sixteenths and 100 is a sixteenth-note triplet shuffle** ([Danielsen et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/popular-music/article/shaping-rhythm-timing-and-sound-in-five-groovebased-genres/BBC410F9849DB982AEBFACEA14D38F32)). Converting — researcher's arithmetic, not a sourced claim — 22–40% of the way from 50% to 66.7% is **53.7–56.7% on the MPC scale**, which lands almost exactly on Attack's Detroit figure and just above its Chicago one. That is a real if loose convergence between a magazine recipe and an interviewed producer, and it is the closest thing to corroboration available. By the same conversion, garage's 70–80% MPC would be roughly 150–225% Ableton, i.e. well past a triplet shuffle — consistent with garage being the one genuinely shuffled style.

Three structural contrasts matter more than the numbers. First, **scope**: house and techno swing is applied to the sixteenth-note hat layer over a straight eighth-note skeleton — "most hits up to this point fall on eighth notes, and the 16th-note hats are where the swing setting (55–60%) takes effect" ([Belleville Techno](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/belleville-techno/)) — whereas new jack swing swing reinterprets the whole grid. Remove swing from a house pattern and you still have a working house pattern; remove it from a new jack swing pattern and the genre is gone. Since everything in NeoBeat is currently swung globally, **per-lane swing depth is probably a feature requirement rather than a preset setting** for these genres. Second, **velocity substitutes for timing**: the straightest style gets its groove from alternating velocities on straight sixteenths, with the Chicago recipe specifying every other hat at lower velocity and timing straight by default, and the garage recipe adding closed hats "with varying velocities to create swing" ([80s-Style Chicago House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/80s-style-chicago-house/); [90s Jersey Garage House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/90s-jersey-garage-house/)). Third, **the direction of micro-timing flips**: the garage instruction is to push the clap a few milliseconds *ahead* of the kick, with a larger forward push on beat 4 of bar 2 for the "jack" ([90s Jersey Garage House](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/90s-jersey-garage-house/)). That is a per-voice static offset, not swing, and presets should expose the two separately. No number is given for the larger push.

The bass logic is where the two idioms differ most instructively. New jack swing leaves room for the bass by **placing the kick sparsely**; house and techno place the kick everywhere and then **duck the bass out of its way**. The documented constraint is subtractive and easy to enforce in a randomiser: the kick "owns roughly the first 80–100 ms of each beat," and **any bass note landing on a kick beat should be deleted** — forbidding steps 1, 5, 9 and 13 and allowing the rest, with the example pattern hitting steps 2, 7, 11, 14 and 15 ([Myloops, Tech House Basslines](https://www.myloops.net/creating-tech-house-basslines)). The same source specifies a two-layer bass with different settings per layer — sub 1/8 to 1/4 note long, 30–90 Hz, ducked 6–10 dB with attack 0–1 ms, ratio 4:1 to 8:1 and release 80–150 ms; mid/pluck 1/16 to 1/8, 100–600 Hz high-passed at 100–120 Hz, ducked only 2–3 dB, swung 15–25% on Ableton's MPC 1/16 groove, velocities 80–120 — with pitch content limited to root, fifth or minor third and an octave jump every 4 or 8 bars ([Myloops](https://www.myloops.net/creating-tech-house-basslines)). Only the mid layer gets swing and velocity variation. Critically, **these are contemporary tech-house recipes and must not be presented as 1980s Chicago or Detroit practice** — sidechain compression in this form postdates both — and there are no sourced register or octave figures for *classic* house or techno bass at all. The TB-303, for its part, is a performance idiom rather than a note grammar: the acid sound came from **moving the knobs while the machine ran its sequence**, which Phuture were first to do, over a machine whose native unit is **64 one-bar patterns** ([Attack, TB-303](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/hardware-focus/roland-tb-303/)). That source does not state the steps per pattern and does not cover slide/glide, note-length behaviour or register, so the near-universal "16 steps with slide and accent" account is unconfirmed here.

Harmony and arrangement complete the contrast. House chords are 7ths and 9ths — Am7, Dm7, G7, Cmaj7, with Am9 as Am7 plus the ninth — with **extensions placed high above a low bass root so that "close intervals do not crowd the sub region,"** the fifth droppable in dense voicings, a few chord attacks placed *between* the quarter-note kicks, the figure repeated then left with a gap, and Am7–Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 as the eight-bar default with one inversion changed in bars 5–8 ([Mixed In Key](https://mixedinkey.com/captain-plugins/wiki/house-music-chords/)). This is **the weakest-sourced area in the entire assignment**: Mixed In Key is a plugin vendor's marketing wiki, it gives no step positions, note lengths, velocities, octave numbers or register ranges, no academic or primary material on house chord voicing was found, and the universally repeated "stab on the offbeat eighth" convention has **no citation behind it here**. The organ-versus-piano distinction is likewise unaddressed by any source, so the "Chicago organ stab" as a named idiom is unsourced; the only chordal detail Wikipedia offers for house is gospel-influenced piano riffs in garage and Jersey house ([House music, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_music)). Since both idioms use 7ths and 9ths, **extension choice is not a discriminator** — placement and register are. Techno goes further and removes harmonic function: melody and vocals are uncommon and harmonic conventions are often ignored in favour of repetitive note sequences ([Techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)), which licenses a chord lane that holds one chord for 32 bars. Arrangement runs in **16- and 32-bar blocks because DJs count in phrases**, with intro and outro 32–64 bars (under 16 is too short to mix in), breakdowns under 32 bars, one parameter moving over 16–32 bars before each boundary and resetting at it, one crash per 32-bar boundary, and the explicit judgement that **a drum fill every 8 bars is too frequent** ([Tracksensei](https://tracksensei.com/blog/how-to-arrange-a-techno-track)) — the exact inversion of new jack swing fill density, and a clean numeric dial. Club mixes run **7–8 minutes against about 3.5 for a radio edit**, with sections built on repeated eight-bar phrases ([House music, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_music)). Note the mild conflict there: Wikipedia describes house in song vocabulary (intro, chorus, verse sections, midsection, short outro) while the techno literature describes a loop-overdub method, and both are reported rather than reconciled. Finally, the one documented route to funk inside techno without drifting toward new jack swing: early Detroit techno built **polyrhythmic layers from other drum-machine voices on top of the basic disco pattern**, and this syncopated funkiness is what distinguishes the Detroit strain from later, mostly unsyncopated commercial techno ([Techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)). That is extra percussion lanes in cross-rhythm, not swing and not kick displacement. Worth keeping Derrick May's caution beside it, since he states both halves in one breath: "there is no recipe, there is no keyboard or drum machine which makes the best techno," and "The 808 was our preference" ([Techno, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno)).

## Conclusion

The honest shape of this research is that new jack swing is specified by a **per-lane timing policy** rather than by a swing value, which is good news for a preset library and bad news for anyone hoping to type one number into a knob. The documented pieces — even-sixteenth swing on the 50–75% scale, displacement concentrated at the tail of the bar, the backbeat snare treated as its own timing object with beat 2 late and beat 4 straight, swing density on the hat line deciding how much of the global setting is audible at all, quantised kick and snare against hand-played keys and unquantised fills — assemble into a coherent system even though **no single source assembles them for this genre**. The two genuinely load-bearing numbers that do transfer are not rhythmic at all: the **20–30 millisecond window** in which a sound's identity lives, which tells you where preset variants should differ, and the **2.5-second and 13.1-second memory ceilings**, which tell you that one-shots in this idiom end abruptly mid-decay for reasons that had nothing to do with taste. Those two rules are entirely sonics-independent, which makes them the strongest foundation in this report for keeping the structure while replacing everything audible.

Three things should be treated as open questions rather than filled in. Tempo is genuinely undocumented, so any range in the presets has to come from your own measurement of a named track list, declared as such; your 118 BPM working floor is a usable starting hypothesis but it is yours, not a finding. The stab grid is the highest-value unknown: Riley's "16 triplets and then one 16" points at a non-uniform, per-step template that no stock swing control reproduces, and building it as a separate lane with its own editable offsets is the most likely route to the thing that reads as new jack swing when the sounds have all changed. And on the house and techno side, the quantified figures rest on craft convention rather than published measurement, so they belong in presets as defaults you then trust your ears over — with one hard caution worth repeating, since conflating the two swing scales is the single way to get badly wrong presets out of otherwise good data: **50% means straight on the MPC scale, 0% means straight on the Ableton/FL scale, and the conversion between them is not an addition or subtraction of 50.** If one crossover slot is needed between the two libraries, shuffled garage house is it — 70–80% swing on the MPC scale, a clap pushed early, gospel piano content — the one point where the new presets and the existing ones meet.
